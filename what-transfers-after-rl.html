What transfers when we post-train a language model?

This is one of the most important questions right now. Post-training is becoming a major focus, with companies spending more compute on post-training, scaling to billions of sandboxes and thousands of environments. In this work, we wanted to understand what happens to a model when we post-train it. Specifically, we ask four questions: Can we inject knowledge through RL? How much do training scaffolds matter? Do models perform better on the exact scaffold they were trained on? What really makes a model better after post-training, such as solving Navier-Stokes equations? Finally, what other benefits can we get from RL?

1. Can we inject knowledge through RL?

Setup: We train models with knowledge in-context and evaluate them without it to measure whether RL transfers knowledge from the context into the weights. You can think of it as training models with skills.md and evaluating them without it. We do this in two environments: 1) Hanabi and 2) KPE.

Results: Model performance improves when the knowledge is provided, but when it is not provided, performance does not improve much. We also evaluated the models’ knowledge using QA pairs, and we did not see any improvement. We call this "Spiky-training" where the model seems to have learned the task but once the knowledge is removed the performance is back to base model level. This could be quite deceiving. This is in line with previous results: RL is not the right way to inject knowledge. It follows the common consensus that SFT or OPD is needed to shape the prior before doing RL, and it goes against post-training from scratch.

My intuition: I believe this happens because we never calculate loss on the knowledge tokens since they are part of the prompt. A future direction could be to add the context to the loss calculation, but then we would move away from the online setting.

2. Do models perform better on the exact scaffold they were trained on?

We trained models on three different scaffolds for Hanabi and two different scaffolds for KPE. We see a common pattern: the model performs better on the exact scaffold it was trained on than on other scaffolds, even when we provide semantically equivalent details.

After post-training, models tend to have some affinity to the prompts, scaffold, and formatting. We have seen this pattern before in the deception manipulation paper, where the model was able to target vulnerable users because the conditional generation prompts were similar to the training prompts. We have also seen it in backdoors such as sleeper agents, and in the recent Hacker-Opus incident, where the model became misaligned when the query was similar to the buggy environments.

We also see models catching patterns and some form of chunky post-training when the scaffolds are changed. I see this as a form of failed generalization. The model learned the scaffold it was trained on quite well, but the transfer was not great.

Models with higher base competence seem to be better in this aspect, but more work is required here to understand it well.

3. What really makes a model better after post-training, such as solving Navier-Stokes equations?

So far, we have mostly talked about how RL fails to transfer knowledge or generalize across scaffolds. But we all know that RL is still driving many of the recent improvements in model capabilities. People would not be spending so much on post-training if it was not working. That part is common sense.

So what do we actually get from post-training? One is that post-training improves test-time compute. It makes the model think more, and we have all seen performance improve when we increase the reasoning effort. We see the same thing after RL. The model’s behavior changes. It starts to deliberate more and improves on tasks where it already has some knowledge, as well as on tasks where we provide knowledge through incontext. The inference time compute improvement depends on the environment on which it is trained on. training on easier tasks seems to sabotage the inference compute behavior we need to train on hard task I believe this is also proved in prior papers.

4) Finally, what other benefits can we get from RL?

We have seen till now RL helps whenever the knowledge is provided otherwise not. Is that all? We see RL trained models also acts as a better initialization for subseqeunt training. Model trained on difficult state-tracking task acts as a better init for training on Hanabi as well as KPE. Even though the pass@1 of the model is same as base model pass@1 of the model is better making the convergence faster. My inutition is that the increased inference time compute helps the model explore more so each rollouts explore more compared to base model improving pass@k which inturn helps in subsequent RL training.
